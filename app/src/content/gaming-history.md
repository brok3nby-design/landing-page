# My Gaming History
*The games I grew up with, the ones that stuck with me, and why I still love gaming today.*

## Where It All Started

I've been playing video games for pretty much as long as I can remember. I'm almost 47 now, and when I look back, there are certain games that have always stuck with me. Some because they were genuinely amazing, some because they introduced me to something I'd never experienced before, and others simply because of the memories I have of playing them.

I don't necessarily remember everything in the right order, and there are plenty of games I've forgotten the names of. But I remember how they made me feel, what made them special, and why I spent so many hours playing them.

I've played all sorts of games over the years, but a few genres have always been a big part of my gaming history. First-person shooters, point-and-click adventures, and, more recently, management and automation games.

Each one has given me something different.

## The Early Days of Point-and-Click Adventures

Some of my earliest gaming memories are actually from point-and-click adventure games.

I remember being a young kid, probably six or seven years old, visiting someone's house with my family. I can't remember exactly whose house it was, whether it was a relative or a family friend, but I remember sitting at a computer and playing a game with another kid.

I think the game might have been set sometime in the colonial era, or at least that's how I remember it. I wish I could remember the name because it's one of those memories that's always been stuck in the back of my mind.

I remember the experience more than the actual game. Sitting there, exploring, trying to figure things out, and discovering that a video game could be something other than just moving around and shooting things.

That was probably one of my first experiences with adventure games, and it started something that would stick with me for decades.

### Maniac Mansion

One of the earliest point-and-click games I can clearly remember is **Maniac Mansion**.

What a strange, wonderful game that was.

I don't remember every puzzle or everything that happened in it, but I remember how different it felt. You were exploring this weird mansion, interacting with things, figuring out puzzles, and dealing with all these strange characters.

There was something about that style of game that I really enjoyed.

I even ended up getting a copy of Maniac Mansion for the NES years later. Not the original copy I played, obviously, but a nice little collectible to have from that time in gaming.

There was also Day of the Tentacle, which came later, and that whole style of weird, funny adventure game was something I really enjoyed.

### The Sierra Games

Then there were the Sierra adventure games.

Police Quest, King's Quest, Space Quest, and all the others.

These games were a huge part of my early PC gaming years.

**Police Quest** was one of the first ones I remember getting into. It was a little more serious than the others, and I liked that. You had to actually pay attention to what you were doing, follow procedures, and figure things out.

But King's Quest and Space Quest were probably more my style.

They had that sense of adventure mixed with some really good humour. Especially Space Quest. I loved how those games didn't take themselves too seriously.

You could make mistakes, die in ridiculous ways, or discover something completely unexpected just by trying something stupid.

And I liked that.

I've always enjoyed games that have a bit of personality. Games that aren't afraid to be strange, funny, or just a little ridiculous.

The Sierra games did that really well.

### Monkey Island

Of course, I can't talk about point-and-click adventures without mentioning **The Secret of Monkey Island**.

That whole series was fantastic.

The humour, the characters, the puzzles, the dialogue. It was just such a great combination.

Those games had a particular kind of humour that really worked for me, and I think that style has stuck with me ever since.

I still appreciate games that manage to be genuinely funny without trying too hard.

### The Adventures of Willy Beamish

Another game that has always stayed with me is **The Adventures of Willy Beamish**.

I can't even explain exactly why this one stands out so much.

I just remember playing it and having a great time.

It had that cartoon look, the quirky characters, and the kind of strange situations you could get yourself into. It felt different from a lot of the other adventure games I had played.

There's just something about Willy Beamish that I remember fondly, even all these years later.

It's one of those games where I don't necessarily remember every detail, but I remember enjoying the experience.

And sometimes that's what makes a game memorable.

### Leisure Suit Larry and the Other Adventures

Then, of course, there was **Leisure Suit Larry**.

That was a different kind of adventure game, especially when you're a kid playing something you're probably a little too young to be playing.

It was almost like discovering a dirty magazine.

Obviously, that made it pretty interesting at the time.

But underneath all that mature humour, it was still another one of those Sierra-style adventure games where you explored, solved puzzles, and tried to figure out what to do next.

There were so many great games from that era, and I know I'm forgetting quite a few of them.

Even after I started getting more into consoles and other types of games, I never really lost my appreciation for point-and-click adventures.

I still play them today.

There are probably hundreds of them I haven't tried, even though I've always considered myself a fan of the genre.

But whenever I come across a good one, especially something with interesting characters, clever puzzles, or a strange sense of humour, it immediately reminds me why I liked them so much in the first place.

## Discovering First-Person Shooters

Another huge part of my gaming history was first-person shooters.

This was especially true during my later grade-school and high-school years, when PC gaming and multiplayer games started becoming a much bigger thing for me.

There's something about those early days of multiplayer gaming that was just incredible.

Today, playing against other people online is completely normal. But back then, it was a big deal.

Being able to run around a map and shoot at your friends, or compete against other players, was something special.

### Doom and the Early Shooters

One of the first FPS games I remember playing was **Doom**.

I can't remember whether I was playing it multiplayer at first or just single-player, but Doom was definitely one of those early PC games that left an impression on me.

Running around those levels, shooting monsters, finding weapons, and exploring all these strange environments was just great.

There was another game from around that time where I think you played as some kind of sorcerer or wizard. I can't remember the name right now, but I remember it being another early shooter that I enjoyed.

One thing I remember being impressed by in an early FPS was that when you shot a wall, the bullet holes would actually stay there.

That sounds like such a small thing now, but at the time I thought it was amazing.

It was one of those little details that made you realize how much games were changing.

### Duke Nukem

Then came **Duke Nukem 3D**.

That was another fantastic game from those early FPS years.

I remember the multiplayer more than anything.

Running around, finding weapons, blowing things up, and trying to kill your friends before they killed you.

It was just fun.

The game had a lot of personality, too. Duke himself, the ridiculous weapons, the environments, all the little things you could interact with.

It was over the top, and that was part of what made it so good.

Those early multiplayer experiences were some of the most enjoyable gaming moments I can remember.

## Unreal Tournament: One of the Greats

If I had to pick one FPS that really stood out during those years, **Unreal Tournament** would absolutely be near the top of the list.

I played a lot of Unreal Tournament.

A lot.

It was fast, chaotic, competitive, and just incredibly fun.

There was one map I remember that had two towers connected by a long bridge. I believe it was called Facing Worlds.

That was a classic.

But the maps I probably enjoyed the most were the giant-room maps.

These were maps where everything around you was enormous, and your character was tiny.

You might be fighting inside a kitchen, a living room, or some other ordinary environment, except you were about the size of an ant.

A table was suddenly enormous. A chair became a massive structure. Everyday objects became part of the battlefield.

I loved those maps.

I remember using the Translocator to move around, teleporting from one place to another, finding different positions, and trying to surprise other players.

There was just something so fun about taking an ordinary environment and turning it into a massive multiplayer arena.

Unreal Tournament was easily one of my favourite online games of that era.

And I still think those giant-room maps were one of the best ideas for multiplayer maps.

## Wolfenstein and Team-Based Multiplayer

Another game I remember really enjoying was one of the Wolfenstein multiplayer games.

I believe it was **Wolfenstein: Enemy Territory**.

What stood out to me was the ability to play different classes.

You could be a medic, for example, and actually help your teammates by reviving them.

That was something I thought was really cool.

Instead of everyone just running around trying to get the most kills, you could actually contribute to your team in different ways.

You could see a teammate go down, run over, revive them, and get them back into the fight.

That kind of teamwork made the game feel different.

It wasn't just about how many people you killed. It was also about what you did to help your team win.

I always appreciated that.

## Call of Duty: The Early Years

Then came **Call of Duty**.

And that was a big one.

The original Call of Duty was one of the best multiplayer experiences I remember having.

At the time, it felt like a big step forward.

The maps, the weapons, the atmosphere, and the way the game played were just fantastic.

One feature I especially remember was the killcam.

Being able to watch how someone had killed you was such a great addition.

Before that, you'd get shot and immediately wonder how the hell somebody managed to hit you.

Of course, everyone who kills you in an online game is obviously cheating, right?

But now you could actually watch what happened.

You could see where they were hiding, how they spotted you, or how they managed to get that shot off.

I loved that.

I remember one particular map that had two buildings across from each other. I can't remember the name of it, but anyone who played the original Call of Duty might know which one I'm thinking of.

I spent so many hours playing that game.

Call of Duty 2 came along afterward, although I don't remember quite as much about that one.

Then, of course, came **Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare**.

That was another incredible game.

It really felt like Call of Duty had reached another level.

The multiplayer was fantastic, the weapons were great, and there was so much to enjoy.

For me, the original Call of Duty and the first Modern Warfare were two of the highlights of the entire series.

## Battlefield: Bigger Maps, Bigger Battles

Around those years, another series became a huge part of my gaming life.

**Battlefield**.

I believe Battlefield 1942 was the first one I really got into.

What immediately stood out was the sheer size of the maps.

Compared to a lot of other shooters, Battlefield felt enormous.

You weren't just running through small maps shooting people. You had vehicles, aircraft, huge open spaces, and battles happening all over the place.

You could fly planes, drive vehicles, or spend your time fighting on the ground.

Sometimes the maps were so big that it felt like it took forever just to find somebody.

But that was part of what made it impressive.

It felt like you were participating in something much bigger than a regular multiplayer match.

### Battlefield Vietnam: One of My All-Time Favourites

One of my favourite Battlefield games, and honestly one of my favourite multiplayer games ever, was the original **Battlefield Vietnam**.

Not the later Vietnam-themed expansion. I'm talking about the original game.

That game was amazing.

The setting was fantastic. You had these huge jungle environments, vehicles, helicopters, and all sorts of different ways to approach a battle.

But one of my favourite things was the music.

You could get into a helicopter or another vehicle, turn on the radio, and start blasting music while flying around the map.

And the best part was that other players could hear it.

I remember flying around in a helicopter with a few other people, with the music playing while we were heading into battle.

It was such a great experience.

You had several players riding together, someone flying, others shooting, and music blasting out as you passed over the jungle.

It just felt incredible.

The atmosphere was so good.

The jungle environments, the vehicles, the soundtrack, the multiplayer battles. Everything came together in a way that made the game really memorable.

I still think about Battlefield Vietnam to this day.

It was one of those games that captured something special, and I wish we could get another experience like it.

### Battlefield 3 and Battlefield 4

Later on, I got heavily into **Battlefield 3** and **Battlefield 4**.

Both were fantastic games.

By then, the graphics had improved massively, the environments were more detailed, and the battles felt even bigger.

The destruction was another thing that really impressed me.

I remember Battlefield 4 having that huge building or tower that could collapse during a match.

Being able to change part of the map like that was awesome.

It made the environment feel like more than just scenery.

I spent a lot of time playing Battlefield 3 and 4, and they were definitely among my favourite multiplayer shooters.

I also tried Battlefield Hardline, the cops-and-criminals version of Battlefield.

It was okay.

Not terrible, but nothing particularly amazing either.

For me, Battlefield 3, Battlefield 4, and especially the original Battlefield Vietnam were the games that stood out.

## Soldier of Fortune and the Little Details

Another shooter I remember enjoying was **Soldier of Fortune**.

That was a really good game, and I'd love to see it make a proper comeback someday.

There's also another multiplayer shooter I remember, although I can't remember its name.

What stood out about it was the information it gave you at the end of a match.

Instead of just telling you who had the most kills or who did the most healing, it highlighted all these unusual little accomplishments that players had achieved during the match.

I thought that was a great idea.

Nowadays, a lot of games seem to focus on the same basic statistics.

Most kills. Most healing. Highest score.

And that's fine, but I don't think those numbers always tell the whole story.

Just because somebody got the most kills doesn't necessarily mean they were the most valuable player.

Maybe somebody else made a really important play, helped the team at the right moment, or managed to pull off something completely ridiculous.

I always liked when games recognized those kinds of moments.

It's the little details like that which can make a game stand out.

## Why Multiplayer Shooters Meant So Much

Looking back, first-person shooters were a massive part of my gaming life.

Doom, Duke Nukem, Unreal Tournament, Wolfenstein, Call of Duty, Battlefield, Soldier of Fortune.

There were so many great ones.

But I think what really made those games special wasn't just the shooting.

It was the experiences.

Playing against friends, discovering new maps, seeing new technology being introduced, figuring out new ways to play, and having those moments that you would remember years later.

Back then, something as simple as persistent bullet holes, a killcam, a giant multiplayer map, or being able to revive a teammate could feel revolutionary.

And those are the things I remember.

Not necessarily how many matches I won or how many kills I got.

I remember the things that made me stop and think, *Wow, that's pretty cool.*

## Management, Automation, and Building Systems

As much as I love first-person shooters and point-and-click adventures, there's another type of game I've really come to enjoy over the years.

Management and automation games.

Games like **Oxygen Not Included** and **Satisfactory**.

I don't know exactly what you'd call my favourite part of these games, but I really enjoy building systems that can eventually run on their own.

There's something incredibly satisfying about putting a bunch of different things together, getting everything working properly, and then watching the whole system do its job.

### Oxygen Not Included

Oxygen Not Included is probably one of the best examples of what I'm talking about.

It's one of those games I always seem to come back to.

I'll stop playing for a while, then eventually get the urge to start another colony and build everything all over again.

What I really enjoy is figuring out how to make different systems work together.

Take oxygen production, for example.

You can build a system that produces oxygen for your colony, but it also produces hydrogen as a byproduct.

Now you've got to deal with that hydrogen.

But instead of just getting rid of it, you can use it to generate electricity.

And that electricity can help power the equipment that's producing the oxygen and hydrogen in the first place.

I love that kind of thing.

You've taken what would normally be a waste product and turned it into something useful.

And if you design the system properly, you can get it running with very little intervention.

Of course, getting there isn't always easy.

You have to think about how much oxygen you're producing, how much your colony needs, how large your base is, and how you're going to distribute everything.

You need pipes, vents, pumps, power, and all sorts of other equipment.

Then you've got to think about how much gas you're moving, whether your equipment can handle it, and whether the system is actually efficient.

There's a lot of planning involved.

And that's exactly what I enjoy.

It's not just about building something that works.

It's about building something that works *well*.

Something that's efficient, reliable, and maybe even a little clever.

And when you finally get everything running the way you wanted, there's a real sense of satisfaction.

### Satisfactory

Satisfactory scratches a similar itch, although it goes about things differently.

Instead of managing a colony, you're building factories, production lines, and huge interconnected systems.

You start with something fairly simple, but before long you're trying to figure out how to move resources across enormous distances, balance production, and keep everything running.

Again, it's that feeling of building something complicated and eventually watching it all work together.

I think that's what draws me to these games.

I like solving the problems.

I like experimenting with different setups.

And I really enjoy getting to the point where I can stand back, look at everything I've built, and watch it all run.

There's something satisfying about creating a little world that functions because of the decisions you've made.

## The Games That Stay With You

When I look back at all the games I've played, it's interesting how certain ones stand out more than others.

Some games I probably played for hundreds of hours, but I can barely remember anything about them.

Others I might not have played nearly as much, but I still remember them decades later.

I remember sitting at somebody's computer as a little kid, discovering adventure games.

I remember the strange humour of Space Quest and King's Quest.

I remember Willy Beamish and Maniac Mansion.

I remember running around those giant-room maps in Unreal Tournament, feeling like I was the size of an ant.

I remember the excitement of seeing a killcam in Call of Duty.

I remember flying over the jungle in Battlefield Vietnam with music blasting from the helicopter.

And I remember the satisfaction of finally getting a complicated system working properly in Oxygen Not Included.

Those are the experiences that have stuck with me.

I think that's what I've always loved about video games.

They're not just something you play and forget.

Sometimes it's a little mechanic, an environment, a character, a funny moment, or an idea that stays with you for years.

And sometimes you don't even know why a particular game made such an impression.

It just did.

I've played a lot of games over the years, and there are still plenty more I want to talk about, especially when it comes to console gaming and some of the other genres I've enjoyed.

But these are some of the games and experiences that helped shape my love of gaming.

And even after all these years, I'm still finding new games that give me that same feeling I had when I was a kid sitting in front of a computer, discovering something I'd never seen before.
